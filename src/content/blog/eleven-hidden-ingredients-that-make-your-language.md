### What's happening under the hood of your language?

The minutiae of language are tedious to study and very few language teachers have any training in it. For your first language, these features are second nature and thus, extremely difficult to acquire in your second so they are largely ignored but, they comprise heavy-lifting in the developing minds of children. You don't remember learning these and you certainly weren't able to declare them when your mind was writing the information to your left side of your brain. Listed below are some of the most important features of language that you don't know you know. They get difficulty rating for the SLA community at large but the difficulty changes based on the influence of your first languages. 
  
1. **Isochrony** - *The metronome of a language* - ![Metronome | Timing your speech](/images/blog/Wittner_metronome.jpg#left-md)This is the unknown variable between Germanic and Romance Languages and the reason new learners sound slightly off when transitioning from one to another. Simply put, it describes speech timing. Romance languages are **Syllable-timed** with each syllable getting roughly the same duration while Germanic languages are **Stress-timed** where only *stressed* syllables get the beat. Difficulty - 9/10    

2. **Phrasal Intonation Contours** - *The melody of a language* - The intonation of phrases and sentences that can emphasize words, signify sarcasm, questioning or other states by rising and falling. Often responsible for certain accents sounding angry, bored or silly. Each language does this drastically different but most languages agree on a few topics like the rising intonation at the end of a question. Difficulty 8/10 

3. **Suprasegmentals** - *Everything else in a word apart from the phonemes* - The pitch, duration, tone and stress on a word are regular and often affect its meaning. English lexical stress comes out different in verbs and nouns; *They re-CORD a RE-cord*. Chinese marks every syllable with a flat, rising, falling or dipping tone; *tāng **Soup** | táng **Candy** | tǎng **To lie down** | tàng **To scald**. Arabic kata-ba كَتَبَ means **He wrote** but holding the first vowel for a longer duration كَاتَبَ means **He corresponded with**. Difficulty 10/10

4. **Allophonic Variation** - *The way a phoneme changes in regular ways* - ![Allophonic Variations of T | T](/images/blog/t.jpg#left-md) The letter T comes out in at least 5 different ways in the words, Tap, sTop, baT, baTTer or banTer. While you might consider all of these the same letter and perhaps even the same sound, your tongue has five different ways of pronouncing each one. Sometimes it follows the physical articulatory methods that naturally arise from speech. Difficulty 3/10

5. **Phonotactics** - *The rules for building words and how those rules are broken* - Some languages don't allow consonants to cluster up like the the word **strengths**, or the famous Georgian word გვრბრდღვნის, pronounced /gvrbrdɣvnis/ with a stunning 9 consecutive consonants. Coming from a native language like, Korean, which doesn't allow consonants to cluster, this would be very difficult to pronounce. Difficulty 9/10

6. **Deixis** - *The way words are anchored to their speaker's perspective* - The fundamental word **I** describes a different person for everyone who uses it, but if that person is Japanese, they might make a subconscious social calculation before using it. There are a few kinds of deixis. Spatial Deixis allows us to signify things that are near with *this* and far with *that*. An added difficulty for many English learners is learning how to apply those proximal concepts to words concepts and words in speech, rather than actual space. Difficulty 6/10

7. **Morphosyntax** - *The rules of how words form and change* - English verbs don't conjugate much. The nouns get a plural -s suffix but that's about it. There are, however, many meaningful prefixes and suffixes that can create words so English loves combining morphemes, or word parts, but much less for verb endings. There also exist strict rules expletive infixing, which is why *abso-fucking-lutely* makes sense and *absolut-fucking-ly* hurts the ears. In general words without much variation of morphosyntax have stricter rules of phrase-level syntax. Difficulty 8/10 

8. **TAM** - *What verbs are able to do* - ![Tense-Aspect-Mood | T-A-M](/images/blog/t-a-m.jpg#right-md) Stands for Tense-Aspect-Mood and deals with the verb forms of a language by adding information directly on the verb form. Tense informs the listener when the action happens, aspect expresses how actions happen and mood is a bit of a catch all for other information like the speaker's certainty of the event, conditionality, obligation, importance, etc... Difficulty 9/10 

9. **Syntax** - *Word order within the sentence* - The defines the order of subject, verb and objects. English has strict rules on this, as well as many formal arrangements of direct and indirect objects, prepositions, and determiners, but it still retains some degree of flexibility to move adverbs and phrases around within sentences. The languages that don't build words by fixing morphemes onto stems, like Chinese have the strictest syntactical order in sentences and moving a word can break the sentence.  Difficulty 6/10
 
10. **Lexical Semantics** - *How words choose partners and collocate* - The words *make* and *do* have similar meanings, but we don't *make homework*, nor do we *do a cake*. These are the natural pairs of words that make collocations and they're second nature for native speakers, but a challenging feature of any language to learn for native speakers that often misaligns with the collocations of other languages. Difficulty 4/10

11. **Morphosyntactic Alignment** - *The order that tracks actors and objects* - In most languages, the subject is the actor of a verb. Passive sentences give us the ability to leave out that actor or shift them to the end but this actor takes a completely different case in Georgian, Basque and Greenlandic these structures shift drastically.  Difficulty 8/10 (11/10 if your first language is Georgian or Basque)
