The Critical Period is a biologically determined phase from birth until early childhood where our phonological framework is built. When you're a baby, the foundations of your language use are set. This theory states that it is not possible to recreate the conditions later in life effectively limiting our ability to learn a second language. On the other hand, many people claim to have acquired nativelike proficiency in late childhood or even as adults.   

It is the most influential theory in the language learning community but a lot of rumors and marketing schemes originate from it as well. So let's dispell some of the common myths of the Critical Period Theory. 

## Myth 1: Young Children Learn Language Effortlessly

Human babies are rather noisy and those noises are very cute when they're happy. After six to eight months, some of those vocalizations become the first sounds in their language. Before learning that objects and feelings have words, they'll have heard some of those words again and again. Those words are silently impressing in the language centers of their brains. In order to learn that their cries can summon caretakers with tasty food, they'll learn cause and effect. Before learning to call for *bottle* they must learn that this object exist somewhere when they're not in their hands. *Cause and Effect*, *Statistical learning* and *Object Perminence* are hard won lessons on the reality of our surroundings that we often take for granted. These precursors to speech will be learned by most babies under the right conditions but they are neither automatic, nor effortless.

## Myth 2: Babies have Super Powers to Hear and Produce Languages

Research on sound perception concluded that very young babies are **Universal Listeners** who can perceive the difference between sounds in langugages they've never heard. That same research established that the developing mind attunes to your native language, making those sounds harder to differentiate. A very young mind has some automata to help you speak, but reusing that process is like aging backwards. Babies are *unspecialized listeners* the same way a microphone is but as you grow up, other developmental processes take over and build on that early input. this has led people to wonder how many native languages  Bilingualism is relatively common and with a third caretaker, three native languages are possible. Certain communitys make it possible for more than 2 extra caretakers of different languages to interact with a child enough that they can continuously foster the development of four or more native languages but there are practical limits as the child goes to school, and starts to weigh the costs of maintaining their multiple languages throughout their lives. 

## Myth 3: Phonological Sensitivity Ends

So, if the doors of perception begin to close as early as six years-old, should we just drop our translation dictionaries and give up? Well, not so fast. While children learn by example, adults have the superpower of learning through books. We also have better concentration and are able to stay on task, keep focused and organize our study sessions. With a bit of background knowledge about articulation, most adults have little trouble replicating native speech. The major difference is that adults don't instinctively turn these speech patterns into habit and generally fall back on our native accent. Lacking the mental fluidity, we should double down on our train methods and treat our new language like a dance, a song or a speech we want to memorize. In this way, we can passively acquire quite a few of the language features that make adult langauge learning so difficult.

## Myth 4: Children Master Language First, then Conscious Thought

You may have heard that consciousness begins around five years old, which is around the time that the critical period tapers off. This myth comes from the folk belief of *Childhood Amnesia* and that our earliest memoies usually trace back to starting Kindergarden. Parents see their young children speaking, playing and learning, but also notice they don't reliably answer questions or remember what they did earlier. Later in childhood, they might discover the child doesn't remember events from those developmental years leading to the assumption that those years were spent in a kind of half consciousness, while they were making sense of the world with words. In those moment, when a parent looks at a picture book and says the words on the page, they're creating linguistic and emotional connections. A childs imagination grows from this in the early years. Graduating from the critical period and starting conscious are an overlapping milestone in two kinds of thinking.

## Myth 5: Adults are Worse than Children at Learning Language

Adults can learn grammar rules, formal patterns phrases and vocabulary much faster and more reliably than children. Children are better at learning pronunciation, connected speech and implicit semantic word mapping. Children also don't rely on their notebooks in the same way as adults but for us, writing down our target language, revisiting it a few times can usually help us recall new words in conversation. A child's mind is sensitive, and given a favorable environment, it will absorb the language there. Adults, on the other hand, often try to learn a language from an app during their work commute at a high stress job. Add to that the anxiety of making mistakes and the socio-cognitive way that language must be learned and it becomes clear that we need to specialize our learning methods.
