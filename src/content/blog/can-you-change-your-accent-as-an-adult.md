Americans usually speak with a throaty **r** sound, like a pirate — _Arrr Matey. I'll use my carrrd._
![Jolly Roger flying over US Flags](/images/blog/pirate.jpg#left-md) Irish people don't like the **th** sounds and use a **t** or **d** sound to start words like *three* or *there* — _tree_, _dere_. *Ze* French often replace those same *th* sounds with *z* sounds. 

Most English speakers would agree that there is no correct accent. The world speaks in hundreds of different ways all over the world. In fact it's rather crude to suggest that *one*[^*] is correct and the others aren't.  Most people's voice reveals their nationality and sometimes their region. It's usually not rude to guess their origin in polite conversation. Most people are proud of their identity and are happy to talk about it.

## How accents form

We acquire our language from our parents, teachers and other members of our community. Your accent marks you at a young age, but it changes with the language that you accquire throughout your life. In early childhood, the phonal, musical and rhythmical elements of your first language are set. Next, the grammar and word order will start to develop. You'll use new vocabulary throughout your life. Your accent will be heavily influenced by this early acquired language. Later in life you're able to take control of your accent, but as a child, you're a product of your environment.

## What the research says

If you're learning English, training your ear and voice to pronounce the words **sit** **seat** and **site** will help you develop a nativelike accent but the later in live you begin, the more difficult it can be. In the early 90s, a lof of research on brain and language development was getting done. At the University of Washington, Patricia Kulh[^kuhl1991] used a computer to generate an /i/ sound like in the word **seat**. Then she also generated dozens more sounds in a gradient to reach the /ɪ/ sound in **sit** or the /æ/ sound like in **sat**.![Phoneme Gradients | A gradient of sounds between vowels](/images/blog/phoneme-gradients.jpg#left-md) She wanted to see if adults would register the gradually changing sounds. She tested adults and babies of various native languages as well as monkeys by playing the sounds and measuring their perception in a variety of ways.![Guy in Magnetoencephalography Machines](/images/blog/GuyInMeg.jpg#right-md) Little babies and monkeys noticed all the changes in the gradient, but older babies and adults weren't able to notice them if the sounds weren't in their native languages. The sounds in our first language seemed to have a kind of gravitational force and made the other recordings sound the same as them. Kuhl called this the **perceptual magnet**. The inability to distinguish certain sounds is called **phonological deafness**. We can look at another study.

# A More Optimistic Study 

As those fancy electronic hats developed, so did the research methods. Adult, second-language learners wore newer, more advances magnetoencephalography machines that scanned their brains and found results that were much more encouraging. As adult language proficiency increases, the brain rewrites its native phonological filters om a different way[^heidlmayr2021]. By training the voice, we expand the neural pathways beyond the sounds of our first languages.

## Practical exercises that rewire your brain

Adults can reprogram phonological deafness by training speech as an exercize. According to the Perceptual Assimilation Model [^best2003], the adult brain no longer hears and writes sounds but writes them from repetition. The specific physical movements of the lips, teeth, and tongue become new neural pathways. Repetition of common and targeted phrases provides the exact sensory feedback the adult prefrontal cortex requires to override entrenched childhood linguistic filters and construct entirely new neural maps for non-native sounds.

> "When I finished University, I was probably a B1 speaker. I could read and understand quite well. I had to study physics and aviation and read quite a bit in English, but the way I was reading was basically reading them in my head in Italian. Me and all my friends would watch movies in English, but we were still socializing in Italian so speaking just became this curious joke. I went to meet ups, and language exchanges to try to improve my English, but it took another ten years, and promotion dangling in front of me if I could reach C1, to motivate me. I learned to train my voice physically, with mouth position and timing and that's made all the difference."
> — Elena, Flight Attendant 

## When it's worth working with a trainer

the B1 plateau is a common resting place for adult learners simply because coursebooks, language classes and grammar studies aren't the part of language that requires a teacher. We need  materials you can practice and memorize, but after childhood, we might feel awkward training our voices. Even a six year old feels too "grown up" to chant nursery rhymes. Ideally, prosedy and pronunciation would be drilled during your language training but it's crucial at an intermediate level if you want to take control of your voice in a new language. 

If you're working with an AI language training app, then you might getting close to physical, targeted feedback. Some of those apps work alright to reprogram your articulatory muscles. Some people are even able to read about the method and reproduce it alone. But it's a bit like doing yoga alone.
