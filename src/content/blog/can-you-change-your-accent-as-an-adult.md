### Where are you from?

Growing up in New York State before leaving the Americas entirely in 2008, I know well that most North Americans speak with a throaty **r** sound, like a pirate — *Arrr Matey. I'll use my carrrd.* This is part of the infamous American accent that anyone, ANYWHERE can recognize. ![Jolly Roger flying over US Flags](/images/blog/pirate.jpg#left-md) A few other famous accents are recognizable from particular sounds, for example, Irish people don't like the **th** sounds and start words like *three* or *there* with **t** or **d**  — _Table for tree over dere in te corner please_. *Ze* French often replace those same *th* sounds with *z* sounds — *but zis only scratches ze surface of what makes ze different accents*. 

Most English speakers would agree that there is no correct accent. The world speaks in hundreds of different ways all over the world. In fact it's rather crude to suggest that *one*[^*] is correct and the others aren't. Nonetheless, people's voice usually reveals where they grew up. It's usually not rude to guess their origin in polite conversation. Most people are proud of their identity and are happy to talk about it.

## How our accents form

We acquire our language from our parents, teachers and other members of our community. Your accent marks you at a young age, but it changes as you continue to learn language throughout your life. In early childhood, the phonal, musical and rhythmical elements of your first language are set. Next, the grammar and word order will start to develop. You'll use new vocabulary throughout your life. Your accent will be heavily influenced by this early acquired language. Later in life you might be able to take some control of your accent, but as a child, you're a product of your environment.

## What the research says

If you're learning English, training your ear and voice to say the right words is useful. Say "I **sit** on a **seat** at the **site**" and you're training pronunciation in context, which will help you develop a nativelike accent but the later you begin, the more difficult it will be. In the early 90s, a lof of research on brain and language development was getting done. At the University of Washington, Patricia Kulh[^kuhl1991] used a computer to generate an /i/ sound like in the word **seat**. Then she also generated dozens more sounds in a gradient to reach the /ɪ/ sound in **sit** or the /æ/ sound like in **sat**.![Phoneme Gradients | A gradient of sounds between vowels](/images/blog/phoneme-gradients.jpg#left-md) She wanted to see if adults would register the gradually changing sounds. She tested adults and babies of various native languages as well as monkeys by playing the sounds and measuring their perception in a variety of ways.![Guy in Magnetoencephalography Machines](/images/blog/GuyInMeg.jpg#right-md) Little babies and monkeys noticed all the changes in the gradient, but older babies and adults weren't able to notice them if the sounds weren't in their native languages. The sounds in our first language seemed to have a kind of gravitational force on similar sounds. Kuhl called this the **perceptual magnet**. The inability to distinguish certain sounds is called **phonological deafness**. We can look at another study.

# A More Optimistic Study 

As those fancy electronic hats developed, so did the research methods. Adult, second-language learners wore newer, more advances magnetoencephalography machines that scanned their brains and found results that were much more encouraging. As adult language proficiency increases, the brain rewrites its native phonological filters in different ways that were similar to practicing a musical instrument[^heidlmayr2021].  By training the voice in this way, we expand the neural pathways beyond the sounds of our first languages.

## Practical exercises that rewire your brain

Adults can reprogram phonological deafness by training speech as an exercize. According to the Perceptual Assimilation Model [^best2003], the adult brain no longer hears and builds neurons but builds them from repetitive practice. The specific physical movements of the lips, teeth, and tongue become new neural pathways. Repetition of common and targeted phrases provides the exact sensory feedback the adult prefrontal cortex requires to override entrenched childhood linguistic filters and construct entirely new neural maps for non-native sounds.

> "When I finished University, I was probably a B1 speaker. I could read and understand quite well. I had to study physics and aviation and read quite a bit in English, but the way I was reading was basically reading them in my head in Italian. Me and all my friends would watch movies in English, but we were still socializing in Italian so speaking just became this curious joke. I went to meet ups, and language exchanges to try to improve my English, but it took another ten years, and promotion dangling in front of me if I could reach C1, to motivate me. I learned to train my voice physically, with mouth position and timing and that's made all the difference."
> — Elena, Flight Attendant
