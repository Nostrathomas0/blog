Americans usually speak with a throaty **r** sound, like a pirate — _Arrr Matey. I'll use my carrrd._
![Jolly Roger flying over US Flags](/images/blog/pirate.jpg#left-md) Irish people don't like the **th** sounds and use a **t** or **d** sound to start words like *three* or *there* sounding like _tree_ and _dere_, while *ze* French often use *z* and *s* sounds, _mais zis is just ze tip of ze iceberg_. 

Most English speakers would agree that there is no correct accent. The world speaks in hundreds of different accents representing the different regions that people come from. In fact it's rather crude to suggest that *one*[^*] is correct and the others aren't.  Most people's voice reveals their nationality and sometimes their region. It's usually not rude to guess that origin in polite conversation. Most people are proud of their identity and are happy to talk about it.

## How accents form

We acquire our language from our parents, teachers and other members of our community. Your accent marks you as a member of that community, but your voice changes as you age. So do the parts of language that you accquire throughout your life. In early childhood, the phonal, musical and rhythmical elements of your first language are set. Next, the grammar and word order will start to develop. You'll use new vocabulary throughout your life. Your accent will be heavily influenced by this early acquired language.

## What the research says

Training your ear and voice to pronounce the words **sit** **seat** and **site** will certinly help you develop a nativelike accent but there are biological limits. For example,in the early 90s, a lof of research on brain and language development was getting done. At the University of Washington, Patricia Kulh[^kuhl1991] used a computer to generate an /i/ sound like in the word **seat**. Then she also generated dozens more sounds in a gradient to reach the /ɪ/ sound in **sit** or the /æ/ sound like in **sat**.![Phoneme Gradients | A gradient of sounds between vowels](/images/blog/phoneme-gradients.jpg#left-md) She wanted to see if adults would register the gradually changing sounds. She tested adults and babies of various native languages as well as monkeys by playing the sounds and measuring their perception in a variety of ways.![Guy in Magnetoencephalography Machines](/images/blog/GuyInMeg.jpg#right-md) Little babies and monkeys noticed all the changes in the gradient, but older babies and adults weren't able to notice them if the sounds weren't in their native languages. The sounds we're conditioned seemed have a kind of gravity. Kuhl called this the **perceptual magnet**. The sound pulled the nearby sounds towards it and she concluded that was a non-reversable change in the language center of the brain.

This study is based in accoustic tests and represent a challenge, not a limitation. To over come what has been termed **phonological deafness** we can look at another study.

# A More Optimistic Study 

As those fancy electronic hats developed, so did the research methods. Heidelmeyer put adult, second language learners into newer, more advances magnetoencephalography machines over the course of their study and found that as adult language proficiency increases, the brain would rewrite it's native phonological filters[^heidlmayr2021], rewriting exactly the mechanism that trained the brain to the sounds of our first languages.

## Practical exercises that rewire your brain

Adults can reprogram phonological deafness by training speech as a task. According to the Perceptual Assimilation Model [^best2003], the adult brain no longer hears and writes sounds but writes them from repeated excercize. The specific physical movements that the lips, teeth, and tongue make become new neural pathways. This is why the the language-lecture class model was largely abandoned and gave rise to the teacher-as-trainer method. The proactive, physical approach provides the exact sensory feedback the adult prefrontal cortex requires to override entrenched childhood linguistic filters and construct entirely new neural maps for non-native sounds.

> "When I finished University, I was probably a B1 speaker. I could read and understand quite well. I had to study physics and aviation and read quite a bit in English, but the way I was reading was basically reading them in my head in Italian. Me and all my friends would watch movies in English, but we were still socializing in Italian so speaking just became this curious joke. I went to meet ups, and language exchanges to try to improve my English, but it took another ten years, and promotion dangling in front of me if I could reach C1, to motivate me. I learned to train my voice physically, with mouth position and timing and that's made all the difference."
> — Elena, Flight Attendant 

## When it's worth working with a trainer

the B1 plateau is a common resting place for adult learners simply because coursebooks, and language classes want to teach you relatable content — materials you can practice and memorize, but after childhood, we might feel awkward training our voices. Even a six year old feels too "grown up" to chant nursery rhymes. Ideally, prosedy and pronunciation would be drilled during your language training but it's crucial at an intermediate level if you want to take control of your voice in a new language. 

If you're working with an AI language training app, then you might getting close to physical, targeted feedback. Some of those apps work alright to reprogram your articulatory muscles. Some people are even able to read about the method and reproduce it alone. But it's a bit like doing yoga alone.
