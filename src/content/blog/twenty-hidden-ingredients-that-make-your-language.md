### What's happening under the hood of your language?

The minituae of language. These features are second nature and thus, extremely difficult to acquire. Most of them are largely ignored in Second Language Acquisition and in First Language Acquisition, they comprise real, heavy-liftin in the developing minds of children. You don't remember learning them. 
  
1. Isochrony - The metronome of a language and the arch difference between Germanic and Romance Languages. Simply put, it describes speech timing. Romance languages are **Syllable-timed** with each syllable getting roughly the same duration while Germanic languages are **Stress-timed** where only *stressed* syllables get the beat. Difficulty - 8/10

2. Phrasal Intonation Contours - The rising and falling intonation of phrases and sentences that can emphasize words, signify sarcasm, questioning or other states. Often responsible for certain accents sounding angry, bored or silly. Each language does this drastically different but most languages agree on a few topics like the rising intonation at the end of a question. Difficult 6/10 

3. Suprasegmantals - The pitch, tone, stress and duration in words; also known as prosody or the other components of a word apart from it's phonemes. English lexical stress can change meaning; *They re-CORD a RE-cord* Chinese marks every syllable with a flat, rising, falling or dipping tone; *tāng **Soup** | táng **Candy** | tǎng **To lie down** | tàng **To scald**. Arabic kata-ba كَتَبَ means **He wrote** but holding the first vowel for a longer duration كَاتَبَ means **He corresponded with**. Difficulty 7/10

4. Allophonic Variation - The way a phoneme can change in a regular way, ex. the letter T comes out in at least 5 different ways in the words, Tap, sTop, baT, baTTer or banTer. While you might consider all of these the same letter and perhaps even the same sound, your tongue has five different ways of pronouncing each one. Sometimes it follows the physical articulatory methods that naturally arise from speech. Difficulty 5/10

5. Phonotactics - The rules to make up words in a particular language. Some languages dont' allow consonants to cluster up like the the word **strengths**, or the famous Georgian word გვრბრდღვნის, pronounced /gvrbrdɣvnis/ with a stunning 9 consecutive consonants. Coming from a native language like, Korean, which doesn't allow consonants to cluster, this would be very difficult to pronounce. Difficulty 9/10

6. Deixis - The way words are anchored to their speaker's perspective. The fundamental word **I** describes a different person for everyone who uses it, but if that person is Japanese, they might make a subconscious social calculation before using it. There are a few kinds of deixis. Spacial Deixis allows us to signify things that are near with *this* and far with *that*. An added difficulty for many English learners is learning how to apply those proximal concepts to words concepts and words in speech, rather than actual space. Difficulty 6/10

7. Morphosyntax - The rules of how words form and change. English verbs don't conjugate much. The nouns get a plural -s suffix but that's about it. There are, however, many meaningful prefixes and suffixes that can create words. There also exist strict rules explitive infixing, which is why *abso-fucking-lutely* makes sense and *absolut-fucking-ly* hurts the ears. In general words without much variation of morphosyntax have stricter rules of phrase-level syntax. Difficulty 8/10 

8. TAM - Stands for Tense-Aspect-Mood and deals with the verb forms of a language by adding information directly on the verb form. Tense informs the listener when the action happens, aspect expresses how actions happen and mood is a bit of a catch all for other information like the speaker's certinty of the event, conditionality, obligation, importance, etc... Difficulty 6/10 

9. Syntax - Word order within the sentence. The defines the order of subject, verb and objects. English has strict rules on this, as well as many formal arrangements of direct and indirect objects, prepositions, and determiners, but it still retains some degree of flexibility to move adverbs and phrases around within sentences. The languages that don't build words by fixing morphemes onto stems, like Chinese have the strictest syntactical order in sentences and moving a word can break the sentence.   
 
10. Lexical Semantics - How words prefer certain partners to others. The words *make* and *do* have similar meanings, but we don't *make homework*, nor do we *do a cake*. These are the natural pairs of words that make collocations and they're second nature for native speakers, but an challenging feature of any language to learn for native speakers that often misaligns with the collocations of other languages.
