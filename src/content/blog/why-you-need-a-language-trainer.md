Language learning is like learning a sport. You started English in grade school and you were good at it. Your teachers encouraged you to continue, so you kept learning through university. You chose a degree with some English instruction, and again a teacher encouraged you onward. Maybe your program even included some proficiency-exam prep — TOEIC or IELTS — and you took it once, then again.

## The problem with "a few hours a week"

Those teachers had your attention for a few hours a week. Some gave homework. The best ones even checked it, dedicating real professional time to giving you feedback. But a few hours a week, spread across years, isn't how skills that require muscle memory actually get built.

## Why "trainer" is the more honest word

Let me lead with the answer: learning a language is muscle memory — closer to training for a sport than to studying a subject. Kids can learn it, in some ways more efficiently than adults. It's also one of the few skills genuinely enhanced by socializing (and, for what it's worth, some learners report a drink or two loosens the tongue — not a recommendation, just an observation about how social and physical this skill really is).

There isn't one single leading pedagogy for language learning. It isn't an exact science, and I'd argue that learning more like children do — through repetition, feedback, and use, rather than abstract study — genuinely helps.
