With everything new coming out this month, it falls to us again to do a tech evaluation. While the big tech companies are locked in their usual arms race of toolsuites, we pulled a few specific products aside to look at what hardware is actually useful for language learning right now.

## 1. Camera/mic glasses — ✅ Try it, with caution

![Camera glasses](/images/blog/gadget-camera-glasses.jpg)

Retailing around €100, these come in first not for their ability to surveil a situation, but to quietly assist it. The idea of spy-style glasses is as old as film itself, but the real value here is mundane: listening to music, then asking an AI assistant to translate a line on the fly, with the outward grace of someone just talking on the phone in public. Use with caution regarding privacy and local recording laws.

## 2. Stick translators — ❌ Skip it

![Stick translator](/images/blog/gadget-stick-translator.jpg)

Good models that recognize spoken language and translate quickly run around €50, but we don't rate these as more than a novelty for real conversation. At any real depth, speaking through an intermediary device gets tiresome fast. Unless you're a strongly auditory learner who mimics pronunciation well, you're better off reading and writing your way through translation tasks.

## 3. Smartphone apps — ✅ Try them

![Language learning apps](/images/blog/gadget-apps.jpg)

Apps are the Tower of Babel of language tools — nearly every known language is represented, for better or worse. Looking at their components helps you pick the right one: spaced repetition suits vocabulary flashcards, sentence-building tests your grasp of syntax, and multiple choice maps well onto grammar questions. Recent apps increasingly integrate AI, offering affordable, reasonably fair feedback on spoken English.

## 4. Tablet/earphone translation suites — ❌ Skip it

![Translation earphones](/images/blog/gadget-translation-earphones.jpg)

Studies consistently show successful language acquisition is more closely tied to study and active practice than to translation convenience. Translation is a legitimate method, but it's the active mental work of comparing meaning and choosing your own words that actually drives learning. A device this specialized belongs with interpreters and international bodies — not with everyday learners.

## 5. AR glasses — ✅ Try it, selectively

![AR glasses](/images/blog/gadget-ar-glasses.jpg)

Heavier, more conspicuous AR glasses have reliably identified objects and scanned text for years, and they're already widely used by hearing- and visually-impaired users — so how do they serve language learners specifically? Immersive tools like Noun Town or Mondly try to simulate a teacher through AR, but a stripped-down AR agent that simply listens for your mistakes and compiles them afterward is, in practice, the more useful version. It's a small trick, but it works.
