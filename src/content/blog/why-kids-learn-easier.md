Nobody remembers learning their first language. We don't remember first recognizing that *mom* is our favorite lady, *water* is the wet stuff that satisfies, or that if you call someone's name, they answer. Every child develops their first language the same basic way — acquired from family and culture, simply part of the world around them. Learning to speak was like learning to walk: a major node on our personal skill tree. To walk, you need balance; to speak, you need to hear and make sounds — and you did both because you're human. Our brains and voices start us climbing the ladder of language immediately, and we keep climbing well into adulthood. But it's those formative years that make our first language special.

## What's different when adults learn a second language

Adults aren't handed the same conditions. We don't get long stretches of play and free time to let our minds develop around a new language, and many of us don't get enough sleep to let the brain self-organize the way a child's does. The core difference is neuroanatomical: a developing brain is uniquely built to mimic and store speech patterns in deep, durable memory. An adult brain, by contrast, is better at learning new skills and disciplines *through the lens of a first language* — which is a different tool, not a worse one.

## What adults can still recreate

All is not lost in adulthood. To a real extent, we can rebuild — and even improve on — some of the conditions children learn under.
