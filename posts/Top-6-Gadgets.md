\#The Top 3 Gadgets for Language Learning, plus 3 more that fail



With all the new stuff coming out this month it falls on us again to do a tech eval. And while the big six digital companies are locked in a fatal array of toolsuites, we take a few products to the sidelines to discover what kind of hardware is being used for language learning right now. 



First up we have the camera/mic glasses, retailing now around 100€ and coming in first because of their ability to surveil the situation but also to disrupt it. And even though this idea is as old as movies, it's not the spy shades we're worried about. It's the convince of listening to music, than asking AI to translate a line that makes this talkie walkie a hit. #1 not only because of it's price, but also framing phone calls with the grace of anyone talking on the phone in public. Use with caution.



Next there is stick translator. A good one that can recognize spoken languages and quickly give a translation are hovering around 50€ but we don't think they're anything more than just a novelty in conversations. At any depth of conversation, speaking through a medium gets tiresome. Unless you're a pure auditory learner and can mimic pronunciation very well, you're likely better off reading and writing to work with translation tasks. Skip it.



Smartphone apps are the Tower in which all languages are known, or rather all known languages are sold. We can rate and rank them in the comments if you please, but by looking at their components, we can start the find the right ones. Spaced Repetition is good for vocab flashcards, Sentence Building asks if you know the proper syntax and Multiple Choice maps well to questions about grammar. Recent apps integrate AI providing affordable, fair quality feedback on your spoken English. Try them.



Some companies are selling tablets/earphones combos with premium translation suites. Studies, however show that successful language acquisition is more closely aligned with study and practice methods, and while translation is certainly one of those methods, it's the active mental task of comparing meaning and finding suitable words that facilitates successful learning. A piece of technology, so specialized to language translation should be used for specialized translation tasks, not learning. These devices belong in the halls of the international governing bodies but the rest of us don't need it.



For a last good one, we wanted to present something unexpected and truly cutting edge, but to save the worst for last nothing that goes under the skin. AR glasses to pick up and put on at the museum add layers of audio/visual input. Heavy, conspicuous glasses have identified common objects and scanned text reliably for five years now, and they're widely used among hearing and/or visually impaired people, so how has this technology served language learners best? Noun Town, Mondley and other immersive software for AR headsets are programmed to simulate the teacher but I prefer a stripped down AR agent that listens for your mistakes and generates a page of them. While these programs are invasive, I don't mind an app being used to record and analyze my voice for errors. It's a neat trick and it works.



Don't wait for Neuralink, and for Pete's sakes don't give Duo the owl access to your dreams. Bio-chipping is usually just a subdermal ID but when it does interact with the nervous system, it's mainly for mobility.
